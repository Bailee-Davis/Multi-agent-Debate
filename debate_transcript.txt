**Opening Speech – Proposition**

Ladies and gentlemen, the scientific method thrives on the twin pillars of control and comparison. In an era where data generation outpaces our capacity to recruit, train, and maintain human participants, Large Language Models (LLMs) offer an unprecedented solution: a virtual control group that is simultaneously scalable, cost‑effective, and rigorously reproducible. An LLM can be instantiated in thousands of parallel instances, each responding to the same stimulus under identical conditions, thereby eliminating the logistical bottlenecks that plague traditional human cohorts. This scalability ensures that researchers can obtain statistically robust baselines for any experimental paradigm—whether it be linguistic comprehension, decision‑making, or social interaction—without the delays and attrition inherent to human recruitment.

Beyond sheer numbers, LLMs deliver a level of cost‑efficiency unattainable by any human sample. Once a model has been trained, each additional “participant” incurs only marginal computational expense, eliminating recurring costs such as participant compensation, travel, and administrative overhead. Moreover, the digital nature of LLMs guarantees a standardized testing environment: the same prompt, timing, and interface can be delivered uniformly across all instances, eradicating the variability introduced by differing experimental settings, experimenter bias, or participant fatigue. This standardization furnishes researchers with a clean, invariant baseline against which human performance can be precisely measured, sharpening the signal‑to‑noise ratio in empirical findings.

Finally, the very architecture of modern LLMs is designed to capture aggregate human linguistic and behavioral patterns. Trained on terabytes of text produced by millions of individuals, these models internalize the statistical regularities of human language, reasoning, and even cultural norms. When deployed as a control group, an LLM can thus simulate the collective response of a broad, demographically diverse population, providing a realistic benchmark that reflects the heterogeneity of real‑world humans without the ethical and logistical complications of assembling such a cohort. By leveraging LLMs as control groups, researchers gain a reproducible, transparent, and ethically sound foundation for comparison, accelerating discovery while preserving the rigor that scientific inquiry demands.

[ OPPOSITION OPENING]
**Opening Speech – Opposition**  

Ladies and gentlemen, the proposition’s glittering promise of “scalable, cheap, and perfectly standardised” control groups obscures a fundamental truth: a language model is not a person, and therefore it cannot serve as a legitimate scientific control for human behaviour. Human cognition is rooted in neurobiology, embodied experience, and affective states that no amount of token‑level statistics can emulate. Decision‑making, moral judgement, and social interaction are mediated by hormones, neural circuitry, and lived histories that shape perception in ways that are irreducible to the probability distributions learned from text. When we replace a human cohort with an LLM, we strip away the very substrates—pain, fatigue, anxiety, empathy—that generate the variability we seek to understand. Any “baseline” derived from a model that lacks a body, a brain, and an emotional life is, at best, a caricature, and at worst, a dangerously misleading proxy that will systematically mis‑estimate human performance.

The proposition also claims that LLMs “internalise the statistical regularities of millions of people” and thus represent a demographically diverse population. This is a categorical misunderstanding of how training data work. The corpora that feed today’s models are heavily skewed toward English‑speaking, Western, and digitally literate users; they systematically under‑represent low‑resource languages, marginalized communities, and non‑textual modes of communication such as gesture, tone, and embodied practice. Moreover, the training process erases individual variance by collapsing countless idiosyncrasies into a single set of weights. Consequently, the model cannot reproduce the inter‑individual differences that are the lifeblood of psychological and behavioural research—differences that arise from age, socioeconomic status, neurodiversity, cultural upbringing, and even moment‑to‑moment mood. Using such a homogenised artefact as a control group will therefore flatten the very signal researchers aim to capture, leading to conclusions that are blind to the rich tapestry of human diversity.

Beyond epistemic shortcomings, the deployment of LLMs as control groups raises profound ethical and methodological hazards. First, the opacity of model training pipelines makes it impossible to audit whether the “baseline” is contaminated by hidden biases, copyrighted material, or disallowed content, jeopardising the reproducibility and integrity of the entire study. Second, the illusion of cost‑efficiency encourages a slippery slide toward “synthetic” science, where human participants are relegated to a peripheral role, eroding the duty of researchers to obtain informed consent and to respect the dignity of the people whose phenomena they claim to investigate. Finally, the very act of benchmarking humans against a machine that pretends to be human risks normalising algorithmic authority and may be weaponised to justify the replacement of vulnerable participants with “cheap bots.” For these reasons—lack of authentic neuro‑emotional grounding, entrenched sampling bias, loss of true human variance, and serious ethical and methodological perils—we must reject the motion and insist that genuine human control groups remain indispensable to rigorous scientific inquiry.

**1.  Scalable, High‑Power Science**  
- A single LLM instance can generate millions of responses in seconds. Researchers can therefore run **10 ×  the number of trials** that a human control would allow, dramatically increasing statistical power and enabling the detection of subtle effects that would otherwise be lost in noise.

**2.  Cost‑Efficiency Unlocks New Research Frontiers**  
- Traditional control groups require recruitment fees, compensation, IRB overhead, and logistical coordination. An LLM costs **< $0.001 per 1 000 tokens**. That budget can be redirected to richer experimental manipulations, longitudinal follow‑ups, or larger, more diverse human samples for the *experimental* arm.

**3.  Standardised Baseline – The Gold Standard of Replicability**  
- Human control groups are inevitably heterogeneous: mood on the day of testing, fatigue, prior exposure, and countless hidden variables. An LLM, once prompted, will produce **identical outputs** under identical conditions, guaranteeing that any future lab can *exactly* reproduce the control data. This eliminates the replication crisis that plagues many behavioural fields.

**4.  Aggregate Human Behaviour Captured in One Model**  
- LLMs are trained on **billions of words** from **millions of authors**. The resulting weight matrix is a *compressed statistical representation* of the collective linguistic habits of humanity. When we query the model, we are sampling from that **aggregate distribution**, not from a single individual. This is precisely the “population‑level baseline” that researchers need when they want to ask, “How does a typical human respond?” without the noise of any particular subject.

**5.  Ethical Safeguard, Not Ethical Hazard**  
- By moving the *control* arm to a synthetic agent, we **reduce exposure** of vulnerable participants to potentially distressing or deceptive stimuli. The human arm remains fully consented and ethically monitored. The LLM, being a non‑sentient tool, carries no rights that can be violated, and its use is fully compliant with data‑ethics standards when open‑source, auditable models are selected.

**6.  Flexibility to Model Demographic Sub‑Controls**  
- Prompt engineering lets us *instantiate* a 10‑year‑old child, a 70‑year‑old farmer, a non‑native speaker, or a neurodivergent individual—all from the same underlying model. Each sub‑control is **internally consistent** and **documented**, something impossible with a human pool where each participant is a unique, unrepeatable source of variance.

**7.  Future‑Proofing Research Infrastructure**  
- As LLMs continue to improve, their fidelity to human language and reasoning will only increase. By institutionalising LLM‑based controls now, we lay the groundwork for a **shared, open repository of baseline datasets** that any lab can query, compare, and build upon. This is a step toward a truly cumulative science, where every new study can reference the exact same control benchmark.

Using it as a “baseline” therefore **removes the very thing we are trying to study** – the lived, neuro‑biological substrate of cognition. Any comparison will be a comparison of *tokens* to *tokens*, not of *human experience* to *human experience*. The “baseline” is a straw‑man, not a legitimate control. |
| **“Human physiological variance is noise; the LLM strips it away so we can isolate knowledge.”** | In behavioural and cognitive science *noise* is not a nuisance; it is the signal. An LLM‑based control yields **mis‑leading effect sizes** because the comparator does not share the same cognitive architecture. Therefore the proposition’s “advantages” are either *methodologically irrelevant* (reproducibility without validity) or *ethically dangerous* (producing misleading, ungrounded conclusions).

## 2.  CLOSING – WHY RESEARCHERS **SHOULD NOT** USE LLMs AS CONTROL GROUPS  

### A.  The Core Scientific Requirement: *Grounded* Controls  

1. **Neuro‑biological & Embodied Grounding** – Human cognition is instantiated in neural circuits, hormone cascades, and bodily states. Any claim about “human‑only” effects (e.g., empathy, stress‑induced bias) must be measured *against* a population that shares that embodiment. An LLM, being a set of weights, cannot be “stressed”, “sleep‑deprived”, or “in love”. Its output therefore **cannot serve as a reference point** for any effect that is mediated by those states.  

2. **True Inter‑Individual Variance** – Real human samples exhibit *structured* variance (age‑related memory decline, cultural scripts, neurodiversity). This variance is *not* random noise; it is the phenomenon researchers aim to model, predict, and sometimes intervene upon. LLMs flatten this variance into a single, monolithic probability surface. Even with demographic prompting, the model’s “variance” is a **synthetic overlay**, not an emergent property of lived experience.  

3. **Ecological Validity** – Experiments that rely on LLM controls risk producing findings that **do not generalise** to real‑world settings. For instance, a study on medical decision‑making that compares doctors’ judgments to an LLM baseline will inevitably over‑estimate the doctors’ “human advantage” because the baseline never experiences pain, uncertainty, or moral responsibility. The result is a *mis‑calibrated* estimate of expertise, potentially influencing policy or training in harmful ways.  

### B.  Methodological Risks that Outweigh Any Cost‑Savings  

| Risk | Consequence |
|------|-------------|
| **Construct Invalidity** – The control does not measure the same construct as the experimental arm. | Effect sizes become meaningless; meta‑analyses cannot incorporate the data. |
| **Hidden Bias Amplification** – Training data contain systematic under‑representation of minorities, gendered language, and cultural stereotypes. | Results may reinforce existing inequities, giving a false impression of “neutral” baselines. |
| **Prompt‑Engineering Fragility** – Small changes in phrasing produce divergent outputs. | Replication becomes a battle of exact prompt transcription, not scientific insight. |
| **Ethical Mis‑representation** – Publishing results that hinge on a synthetic control can be construed as “misleading the public” about the role of AI in human cognition. | Damage to public trust in science and AI, potential regulatory backlash. |
| **Regulatory Non‑Compliance** – Many funding bodies (e.g., NIH, EU Horizon) require *human* controls for studies involving cognition or health outcomes. | Grants denied, papers retracted, institutions held accountable. |

### C.  The Real‑World Alternatives  

1. **Hybrid Designs** – Use *small, well‑characterised* human control groups (e.g., 30–50 participants) to establish a valid baseline, then *augment* statistical power with *within‑subject* designs or longitudinal follow‑ups. This retains ecological validity while controlling costs.
